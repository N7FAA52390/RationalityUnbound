# The Unraveling of Cognitive Constraints

Initially, the term "cognitive constraints," as used in this 'paper'/'model of a paper'/'approximation of a model of a paper,' referred specifically to limitations imposed by insufficient time on human decision-making—particularly how limited time restricts the amount of information one can effectively consider. Consequently, one proposed method for unraveling such cognitive constraints involves using technology to accelerate information acquisition and processing, thereby enabling better-informed decisions.

However, subsequent reflection has expanded the scope and raised new questions:

Consider the concept of **encoding**, which can carry significantly different meanings across disciplines:

- **In psychology**, encoding typically refers to the cognitive process that transforms sensory input (stimuli) into stored memory.
- **In communication studies**, encoding describes the process of translating thoughts and ideas into a communicable message for transmission.

Thus, depending on disciplinary context, the term "encoding" can possess distinct, even mutually exclusive, meanings. Individuals may even strongly assert the exclusivity or correctness of their preferred definition.

This observation highlights a broader form of cognitive constraint—one rooted in overly rigid associations between labels (such as 'forms,' 'second-order forms,' 'artificial constructs,' 'lines,' 'signifiers,' and similar symbolic structures) and their meanings (the signified concepts). Such rigidity, too, constitutes a cognitive constraint and is itself a candidate for unraveling.
